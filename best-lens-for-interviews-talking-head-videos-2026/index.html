Best Lens for Interviews and Talking-Head Videos — A Practical Buying Guide

For interviews and talking-head videos, the best lens is a fast prime or zoom in the 50mm-85mm full-frame equivalent range, ideally f/1.8 or wider, so the face stays flattering and the background falls softly out of focus. Here is exactly which lenses to buy at every budget.

By Ajay K MeenaUpdated: 202610 min read

Every interview setup lives or dies on one lens choice. Lighting can be fixed in five minutes, audio can be cleaned up in post, but a wrong focal length on a talking head is baked into the shot forever — you cannot un-distort a nose that got stretched by a 24mm lens two feet from someone's face. This guide covers the exact focal length range that flatters faces, the aperture you actually need for background separation without losing focus on a moving subject, and specific lens recommendations across three budget tiers.

Quick take: For a single subject sitting reasonably still, an 85mm f/1.8 (full-frame equivalent) is the gold standard for interviews — flattering compression, creamy background blur, sharp focus falloff. If your subject moves around while talking (hand gestures, leaning in and out), drop to a 50mm f/1.8-f/2.8 so your focus plane has more breathing room. Budget shooters should start with a 50mm f/1.8 "nifty fifty" — it is the single best value lens for this job at any price.

Why 50mm-85mm Is the Sweet Spot for Faces

Facial perspective is controlled by camera-to-subject distance, not focal length directly — but focal length determines how close you need to stand to fill the frame the same way, and that distance is what actually distorts or flatters a face.

Shoot an interview at 24mm and you have to get uncomfortably close to fill the frame with a face. At that distance, the parts of the face nearer the lens (nose, forehead) are proportionally much closer to the sensor than the parts further away (ears, back of the head), and the lens exaggerates that difference. The result is a stretched, bulging look that nobody wants in a talking-head video.

Move out to 50mm-85mm and you can frame a tight headshot from a comfortable 5-10 feet away. At that working distance, the difference in distance between the nose and the ears becomes proportionally small, so the perspective compresses and flattens naturally. This is the same optical principle that makes classic portrait photography lean on 85mm and 105mm lenses. Anything from 50mm up through 135mm will read as flattering; 50mm-85mm is simply the practical sweet spot because it still lets you shoot in a normal-sized room without backing into a wall.

What Happens Below 50mm

A 35mm lens is usable for interviews if you are shooting two people in a wider two-shot, or if your room is genuinely too small for anything longer. But for a single tight-to-medium headshot, 35mm starts to add a subtle wideness to the face that most viewers will notice even if they cannot name why the shot feels "off."

What Happens Above 85mm

100mm-135mm lenses give even more compression and a genuinely gorgeous, ultra-flat look — the classic beauty-portrait perspective. The tradeoff is practical: you need 10-15 feet of working distance to frame correctly, which most home studios, offices, and small sets simply do not have. Reserve these focal lengths for bigger rooms or when you are shooting with a longer zoom on a tripod at the back of a space.

Aperture: Background Separation Without Losing the Shot

The aperture question in interview work is really a tradeoff between two things creators want simultaneously: a soft, blurred background (which needs a wide aperture) and reliable focus on a subject who inevitably leans forward, turns their head, or gestures with their whole upper body (which needs a deeper depth of field to forgive small movements).

Here is the practical range that works:

  • f/1.4-f/1.8 — Maximum background blur, best for a subject who sits still and stays roughly the same distance from the lens (studio interviews, podcast-style setups with a fixed chair). Depth of field at these apertures on a tight headshot can be just a few inches, so any real forward-and-back movement will soften focus unless you are using continuous autofocus with reliable face/eye tracking.
  • f/2-f/2.8 — The safest working range for most real-world interviews. Still gives a clearly separated, soft background, but gives you enough depth of field cushion that natural head movement, hand gestures, and breathing don't push the subject out of focus. This is where most professional interview work actually lives.
  • f/4 and narrower — Only worth using if you are lighting a busy or messy background you want to keep somewhat readable (a bookshelf, a workshop, a branded backdrop with logos), or if you are shooting outdoors in bright daylight without ND filters and need to stop down to avoid overexposure.
Pro tip: If your subject moves a lot — leans in for emphasis, turns to gesture at something off camera — shoot at f/2.8 instead of f/1.4 and let your depth of field do the forgiving. A slightly less blurred background that stays in focus always looks more professional than a gorgeous bokeh ball where the subject's eyes have drifted soft.

Autofocus vs. Manual Focus for Talking Heads

Unlike a static product shot, a talking head is a moving target — even a "still" subject sways forward and back by a few inches while speaking. Two workable approaches:

1. Reliable Eye-AF with Continuous Autofocus

Most mirrorless cameras from the last few years have genuinely good human eye-detection autofocus. If your camera and lens combination has fast, sticky eye-AF, this is the easiest and safest way to shoot interviews solo — it will hold focus through natural movement even at f/1.8.

2. Manual Focus with a Focus Assistant

If you are on an older camera, a manual-focus cine lens, or an autofocus system you don't fully trust, stop down slightly (f/2.8-f/4) to buy yourself a deeper focus plane, mark your subject's chair with tape, and pull focus manually or lock it once they are seated. This is the traditional documentary approach and it is completely reliable once your subject agrees to stay roughly in one spot.

Nearly every camera brand makes a 50mm f/1.8 for well under the price of any zoom lens, and it is genuinely the best starting point for interview work. On a full-frame body it gives you the classic "normal" focal length with a comfortably flattering perspective at typical sit-down distances; on an APS-C or Micro Four Thirds body, the crop factor pushes the equivalent field of view closer to 75-100mm, which if anything makes it even better suited to tight headshot interviews.

DOP's take: This is the lens I tell every beginner to buy first, full stop. At f/1.8 it separates a subject from a background beautifully, autofocus is fast because the optical design is simple, and because it's a fixed focal length there is no zoom creep or breathing to worry about mid-interview. The only real limitation is that on a full-frame body you need roughly 6-8 feet of distance to get a tight headshot, so make sure your room has that much depth.

The 85mm f/1.8 is the single most recommended portrait and interview lens across the industry, and for good reason. It gives noticeably more background compression than a 50mm, so a busy office or a cluttered bookshelf turns into a soft wash of color and shape behind your subject, while the face itself renders with almost no perspective distortion at all.

DOP's take: If your interview setup has a fixed, comfortable amount of room — a dedicated studio corner, a rented space, a proper backdrop — this is the lens I would reach for over the 50mm every time. The one caveat: you need genuine distance (8-10 feet minimum) to frame a tight shot, so measure your space before buying. If your room is small, a 50mm will actually serve you better despite the less dramatic compression.

Once you are running a proper multi-camera interview setup — a wide two-shot camera plus a tight single on the subject, or a documentary-style production where you need to reframe without walking across the room — a fast zoom earns its keep. A 24-70mm f/2.8 covers everything from a wider establishing frame down into flattering headshot range, while a 70-200mm f/2.8 lets you sit a camera far back from the subject (useful in small studios where you can't physically stand at 85mm-equivalent distance) and still pull in tight.

DOP's take: This is the setup I use on paid shoots where reframing on the fly matters more than squeezing the absolute maximum bokeh out of a prime. f/2.8 is genuinely enough depth of field cushion for interview work, and the ability to zoom between a medium shot and a tight closeup without swapping lenses or repositioning the camera saves real time on a shoot day. It is a bigger investment, and these lenses are noticeably heavier, so plan on a solid tripod or gimbal rather than handholding for a full interview session.

Matching the Lens to Your Camera Setup

Crop-sensor cameras change this math. On an APS-C body, a 35mm lens already gives roughly a 50-55mm full-frame equivalent field of view, and a 50mm lands closer to 75-80mm equivalent — right in the flattering compression range without needing as much physical room. Micro Four Thirds bodies double the effective focal length again, so a 42.5mm f/1.7 lens behaves like an 85mm on full-frame. Common Mistakes in Interview Lens Choice

Using a Kit Zoom Wide Open

An 18-55mm kit lens at 50mm and f/5.6 will technically frame the shot correctly, but you lose all the background separation that makes an interview look intentional rather than accidental. If a kit zoom is all you have, at minimum zoom to the longest focal length available and get your subject as far from the background as the room allows.

Standing Too Close with a Wide Lens

This is the single most common interview mistake — using a 24mm or 35mm lens and physically walking the camera close to fill the frame. Back up and zoom in, or switch to a longer lens, instead of moving the camera closer.

Ignoring Focus Breathing on Cheap Zooms

Some budget zooms visibly shift their field of view as they rack focus, which is distracting during interviews where you are pulling focus between a wide shot and a close-up. If your zoom exhibits heavy focus breathing, favor primes for your tightest interview shots.

Quick FAQ

Q: What is the single best lens for a one-person talking-head YouTube channel?

A 50mm f/1.8 prime is the best starting point for most creators — it's affordable, gives real background separation, and works on nearly every camera mount. If your room has enough space (8+ feet), an 85mm f/1.8 gives even more flattering compression.

Q: Is 35mm too wide for an interview lens?

35mm is workable for a wider two-shot with two people, but for a tight single headshot it starts to introduce a subtle wideness to facial proportions. 50mm and above is the safer choice for a single-subject interview.

Q: Should I shoot interviews at f/1.4 or f/2.8?

It depends on how much your subject moves. A still subject in a fixed chair can be shot comfortably at f/1.4-f/1.8 for maximum background blur. A subject who leans and gestures is safer at f/2.8, which gives enough depth of field to forgive natural movement without losing focus.

Q: Do I need a full-frame camera for the 50mm-85mm interview look?

No. On a crop-sensor (APS-C) camera, a 35mm lens behaves like roughly 50-55mm equivalent, and a 50mm behaves like roughly 75-80mm equivalent — both land inside the flattering range. Micro Four Thirds cameras double the effective focal length again.

Q: Can I use a zoom lens instead of a prime for interviews?

Yes — a constant-aperture f/2.8 zoom like a 24-70mm or 70-200mm is a great choice for multi-camera setups or when you need to reframe without moving the camera. You give up some maximum background blur compared to an f/1.8 prime, but gain framing flexibility.
