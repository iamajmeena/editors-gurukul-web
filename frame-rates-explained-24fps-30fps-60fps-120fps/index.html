Frame Rates Explained: 24fps vs 30fps vs 60fps vs 120fps

Shoot 24fps for a cinematic look, 30fps for broadcast-style or talking-head content, 60fps for smooth motion or a 2x slow-motion safety net, and 120fps or higher when you need genuine, dramatic slow motion. The frame rate you pick also decides your shutter speed, so get this decision right before you press record.

Quick take: Match your frame rate to your final playback rate, not just to whatever looks smooth in the moment. 24fps is the default for narrative and cinematic footage. 30fps suits fast-turnaround broadcast and some social content. 60fps is your workhorse for real-time smooth motion and a solid 2x slow-motion pull. 120fps and beyond is reserved for footage you actually intend to slow down 4x or more. Whatever you choose, set your shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate — the 180-degree rule — unless you're deliberately breaking it for a look.

What Frame Rate Actually Means

Frame rate is simply how many individual still images — frames — your camera captures every second, expressed as frames per second (fps). Twenty-four frames per second means the camera is recording twenty-four separate images every second, which your eye and brain blend into the illusion of continuous motion when played back. The frame rate you shoot at, combined with the frame rate you deliver at, is what defines whether footage plays back at normal speed, in slow motion, or sped up.

This sounds basic, but frame rate confusion is one of the most common reasons beginner footage looks either "off" in a way viewers can't quite name, or ends up unusable because it was shot at the wrong rate for the intended slow-motion effect. Get the frame rate decision right at the start of a shoot, and a huge category of problems in the edit simply never happens.

24fps — The Cinematic Standard

Twenty-four frames per second has been the standard for theatrical film since the late 1920s, and it has carried over almost unchanged into digital cinema cameras, mirrorless hybrids, and cinema-style YouTube and Instagram content. If you've ever heard someone say a shot "looks like a movie," a large part of that judgment is coming from the motion characteristics of 24fps.

At 24fps, motion has a slightly soft, deliberate quality — fast movement shows natural motion blur rather than crisp, hyper-defined edges. That blur is not a flaw; it's the entire reason 24fps reads as "cinematic" to audiences who have watched decades of film shot this way. It's a learned visual association, but it's an extremely strong one.

  • Use 24fps for: narrative shorts, wedding films, brand films, cinematic vlogs, music videos, and any project where the goal is an emotional, film-like feel rather than crisp documentary realism.
  • Avoid 24fps for: fast sports or action content meant to be watched in real time, and any footage you plan to slow down significantly in post — 24fps gives you almost no room to slow motion down cleanly.

30fps — The Broadcast and Utility Rate

Thirty frames per second (technically 29.97fps in most broadcast-legacy workflows) grew out of television broadcast standards, particularly in NTSC regions like the US, and it remains extremely common for talking-head content, interviews, vlogs, tutorials, and live-adjacent social content. It reads as slightly more "real-time" and less stylized than 24fps — a subtle difference, but one working colorists can usually spot in a blind test.

For a lot of creators, 30fps is a defensible middle ground rather than a wrong choice — it's smoother than 24fps, which can help handheld or gimbal-heavy content feel a touch steadier, without needing the extra light and storage demands of 60fps.

  • Use 30fps for: interviews, corporate and educational content, some YouTube formats, livestream-adjacent recordings, and situations where you're delivering to a broadcast spec that requires it.
  • Avoid 30fps for: footage you're trying to make feel deliberately cinematic — it sits in an odd middle zone that's neither as filmic as 24fps nor as smooth and slow-motion-friendly as 60fps.

60fps — Smooth Motion and Your Slow-Motion Safety Net

Sixty frames per second captures twice the temporal information of 30fps, which makes it the standard choice for two very different jobs: real-time playback of fast motion, and a reliable buffer for modest slow motion in the edit.

Played back at normal speed, 60fps looks noticeably smoother and sharper in motion than 24fps or 30fps — which is exactly why sports broadcasts, action sequences, and some high-energy vlogging content use it. That same smoothness is also why some editors avoid it for narrative work; it can read as "video-like" rather than "filmic" if delivered at native speed without any stylistic justification.

The other major reason to shoot 60fps: if you conform it down to a 24fps or 30fps timeline, you get clean 2.5x or 2x slow motion respectively, with every frame intact and no interpolation needed. This is why experienced shooters often default to 60fps even for scenes they're not sure will need slow motion — it costs you extra storage and light, but it buys you flexibility you can't get back afterward if you shot at 24fps and decide in the edit that a shot needed to breathe in slow motion.

Pro tip: When in doubt on a documentary, wedding, or run-and-gun shoot where you can't re-take a moment, shoot the important dynamic shots at 60fps by default. Cutting 60fps footage into a 24fps timeline at normal speed works fine and just gives you a very slight motion smoothness; the safety net for slow motion far outweighs that trade-off.

120fps and Beyond — True Slow Motion

Once you get to 120fps and higher — many modern mirrorless and cinema cameras now offer 180fps, 240fps, or more at reduced resolution — you're capturing enough extra information to produce genuinely dramatic slow motion: 4x, 5x, or more when conformed to a 24fps or 30fps timeline. This is the rate for the moments an audience is meant to really sit inside — a splash, a hair flip, a punch connecting, a product reveal, a dramatic turn in a narrative scene.

The trade-offs at these rates are real and worth planning around before you shoot:

  • Light requirements go up sharply. A higher frame rate means a shorter interval between frames, which usually forces a faster shutter speed and a smaller effective exposure window — you need considerably more light to expose correctly at 120fps than at 24fps.
  • Resolution or crop factor sometimes drops. Many cameras only offer their highest frame rates at reduced resolution, a sensor crop, or with autofocus limitations — check your specific camera's spec sheet for exactly what you lose at each frame rate before relying on it for a key shot.
  • Storage and card speed demands increase. More frames per second means more data per second; make sure your memory cards are rated fast enough to sustain the bitrate, or you'll get dropped frames or recording stops mid-clip.
  • Audio sync gets trickier in post. Since these clips are almost always conformed to a much slower timeline rate, any usable audio needs to be recorded separately and synced afterward rather than relying on the in-camera scratch track.

Use 120fps+ sparingly and intentionally. A whole film shot in slow motion loses its impact fast — it works because it contrasts with normal-speed footage around it.

The Shutter Angle and Shutter Speed Connection — The 180-Degree Rule

Frame rate doesn't exist in isolation — it's directly tied to shutter speed, and getting this relationship wrong is one of the fastest ways to make footage look wrong without viewers being able to say exactly why. This comes from film cameras, where a physical rotating shutter disc with a cut-out "angle" controlled how long each frame was exposed relative to the frame interval. Digital cameras simulate the same idea through shutter speed or an equivalent shutter angle setting in the menu.

The 180-degree rule is the industry-standard starting point: set your shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate. This produces the amount of natural motion blur audiences are used to seeing and associate with "normal," comfortable motion.

  • Shooting 24fps → shutter speed around 1/48, rounded in practice to 1/50 on most cameras.
  • Shooting 30fps → shutter speed around 1/60.
  • Shooting 60fps → shutter speed around 1/120.
  • Shooting 120fps → shutter speed around 1/240.

If you want to go deeper into how these menu settings interact with picture profiles, frame rate, and exposure on your specific camera body, see our guide to camera menu settings for cinematic footage for a full setup walkthrough.

Why this matters in practice: A faster shutter speed than the 180-degree rule suggests — say, 1/1000 while shooting 24fps — cuts motion blur down dramatically. Every frame becomes crisper and more frozen, which is exactly the "war movie" or hyper-real strobing look you see in combat scenes and some sports coverage. It's a deliberate stylistic choice in those contexts, not a mistake — but if you apply it accidentally to a normal dialogue scene, the motion will look harsh and unnatural, almost stuttery, and most viewers will register that something feels off even if they can't name the cause.

Going the other way — a slower shutter than the 180-degree rule, such as 1/24 while shooting at 24fps — increases motion blur substantially. This can create a dreamy, smeared look useful for specific stylistic moments, but it will make fast motion genuinely hard to read clearly, and it also demands far less light, which changes your exposure math on set.

Matching Frame Rate to Delivery Platform

One detail that trips up a lot of newer shooters: the frame rate you shoot at should ultimately match, or cleanly convert into, the frame rate you deliver at. Shooting 24fps and exporting to a 30fps timeline without a proper conform can introduce judder — a subtle stutter in motion caused by frames being duplicated unevenly to make the math work.

  • If your final output is a cinematic 24fps deliverable, shoot 24fps for your main coverage.
  • If your platform or client specifically wants 30fps (common for certain broadcast or corporate deliverables), shoot 30fps natively rather than converting after the fact.
  • Shoot 60fps or higher specifically for any shot you know or suspect will need slow motion, and plan to conform that specific footage down in your edit software rather than mixing frame rates within a single timeline without proper handling.

Most modern NLEs, including DaVinci Resolve, handle mixed frame rates on a timeline reasonably well through frame rate conforming, but it's still far cleaner to plan your frame rate per shot at the time of shooting than to fix mismatches later.

A Practical Shooting Checklist

  1. Decide your project's final delivery frame rate before you shoot a single frame — this determines your base rate.
  2. Identify shots in your script or shot list that are likely candidates for slow motion, and plan to shoot those specifically at 60fps or higher.
  3. Set shutter speed using the 180-degree rule as your default, and only deviate from it for a specific, intentional look.
  4. Check your camera's crop, resolution, and autofocus limitations at higher frame rates before you rely on them for an important shot.
  5. Confirm your memory cards can sustain the bitrate of your chosen frame rate and resolution combination before a critical shoot day.

Quick FAQ

Q: Is 24fps or 30fps better for YouTube?

Both work, and platforms don't penalize either. 24fps gives a more cinematic feel and is generally preferred for narrative or brand-style content; 30fps suits talking-head, tutorial, and fast-turnaround content that benefits from a slightly more real-time feel. Pick based on the look you want, not platform requirements.

Q: Can I shoot everything at 60fps and slow it down only when needed?

Yes, and many working shooters do exactly this as a safety net, especially on documentary or one-take events like weddings. The trade-offs are higher light requirements, more storage use, and a very slightly smoother, less filmic look at native playback speed — usually worth it for the flexibility.

Q: What happens if I ignore the 180-degree rule?

Nothing breaks technically, but the motion will look either unnaturally crisp and stuttery (faster shutter than the rule suggests) or unnaturally smeared and hard to read (slower shutter). Both can be used intentionally for stylistic effect, but applied by accident they make footage look subtly wrong to viewers.

Q: Why does my slow-motion footage look choppy even though I shot at 120fps?

This is usually a conform issue in the edit — the clip needs to be interpreted or retimed correctly against your timeline's frame rate. Check that your editing software is treating the clip as a slow-motion conform rather than just changing its native frame rate metadata without retiming.

Q: Do I need 120fps if I only want mild slow motion?

No. 60fps conformed to a 24fps timeline already gives you clean 2.5x slow motion, which covers most everyday needs. Reserve 120fps and higher for shots where you specifically want dramatic, extended slow motion.
