Gels and Color Grading on Set: What to Fix In-Camera vs What to Leave for the Grade

Gels are colored filters you place over a light to change its color temperature or add a stylistic tint before the camera ever records the frame. The right philosophy isn't "gel everything" or "fix it all in DaVinci Resolve" — it's getting white balance and base exposure correct on set with gels where needed, then saving the stylized push for the grade, where you have far more precision and far less risk.

Quick take: Use CTB and CTO gels on set to correct or match color temperature between sources — that's a lighting problem, not a color-grading problem, and it's cheaper and more reliable to solve with physical filters than with nodes later. Use effect gels (color-tinted gels in creative hues) when you want a specific colored light source baked into the shot itself, because a real colored light interacting with a real environment looks different from a flat color overlay added in post. Save the stylistic push — contrast shaping, split-tone looks, filmic color palettes — for DaVinci Resolve, where you have far more control and can undo a decision without re-lighting the scene.

What Gels Actually Do to Light

A gel is a heat-resistant colored sheet of polyester or polycarbonate that you clip, tape, or frame in front of a light fixture. The light passes through the gel and comes out shifted toward whatever color the gel is manufactured in. That's the entire mechanism — no electronics, no software, just colored material subtracting certain wavelengths from the light before it ever touches your subject or your sensor.

This matters because it changes color before the exposure happens, not after. A gelled light interacts with skin, fabric, smoke, and reflective surfaces the way real colored light does — bouncing, mixing, and falling off naturally. A color effect added afterward in the grade is a mathematical shift applied uniformly across pixels that were never actually lit that color. The two can look similar at a glance, but under scrutiny — especially in highlights, skin tones, and anywhere light wraps around an edge — a gelled practical light reads as more convincing than a digital tint pushed on top of neutral footage.

CTB and CTO: The Correction Gels Every DOP Should Know Cold

Before you touch a single stylistic gel, you need to understand the two correction gels that solve color temperature mismatches on set: CTB and CTO.

  • CTB (Color Temperature Blue) — shifts a warm light source cooler, pushing it toward daylight. A full CTB on a 3200K tungsten Fresnel pushes it up toward roughly 5600K, useful when you need an interior tungsten unit to match daylight coming through a window.
  • CTO (Color Temperature Orange) — shifts a cool light source warmer, pushing it toward tungsten. A full CTO on a 5600K daylight-balanced LED or HMI pushes it down toward roughly 3200K, useful when an exterior-balanced fixture needs to match interior practicals.
  • Half, quarter, and eighth strengths (1/2 CTB, 1/4 CTO, and so on) — for partial correction. A full-strength gel often overshoots what you actually need, especially when you're nudging a source only part of the way to a target rather than flipping it entirely from tungsten to daylight or back.

The job of CTB/CTO is not creative — it's corrective. You use them so that every light source hitting your subject reads as the same color temperature, which means your camera's white balance setting (or your white balance correction in DaVinci Resolve) only has to solve for one color cast instead of two fighting casts in the same frame. If you've ever tried to correct a shot where the key light is warm and the background window is cold, you already know why this matters — white balance is a single global correction, and it cannot selectively fix one region of a frame without affecting the rest. Getting your Kelvin values to agree on set, using CTB/CTO, is what makes that single correction actually work.

Pro tip: Carry more 1/2 CTO and 1/4 CTB gels than full-strength sheets. In real production, you're almost always nudging a source partway to match another, not flipping it entirely from one extreme to the other. Full-strength gels get used far less often than half and quarter strengths once you're actually matching sources on a live set.

Color Effect Gels: Committing to a Look Before the Grade

Beyond correction, gels come in a full range of creative colors — deep blues, magentas, ambers, greens, and saturated theatrical hues used for mood and style rather than technical matching. These are effect gels, and using them is a genuine creative decision with real trade-offs.

The case for committing to an effect gel in-camera rather than adding a color wash in the grade comes down to three things:

  1. Real light behaves believably. A magenta-gelled backlight rimming a subject's hair, spilling onto a wall behind them, catching in smoke or haze — that's real light doing real things to a real environment. A flat magenta overlay pushed on top of a neutrally-lit shot in the grade can approximate the hue but can't replicate how the color actually falls off, wraps, or mixes with ambient light in the scene.
  2. It disciplines your exposure and contrast on the day. When you commit to a colored key or backlight on set, you're forced to expose and balance the shot with that color already accounted for — which usually produces a more considered, better-separated frame than lighting flat and hoping the color grade will "add mood" later.
  3. It protects highlight and color detail you can't get back. If you light flat and plan to push a heavy color grade afterward, you're relying entirely on the grade to create separation and mood that was never actually captured. Footage that was genuinely lit with intent gives you real tonal information to work with — footage that wasn't gives you a flat base you're forcibly reshaping, which is a much harder and more fragile place to grade from.

The trade-off is real too: color effect gels are a commitment. If the director changes their mind about the mood of a scene after wrap, a baked-in magenta rim light is far harder to undo than a color adjustment layer in DaVinci Resolve. This is exactly why the balanced approach below matters more than a hard rule in either direction.

The Case for Leaving It to the Grade

It would be dishonest to pretend gels solve everything, or that they're always the right call. There are good reasons a working colorist will tell you to leave certain decisions for post:

  • Reversibility. A grade decision can be undone, refined, or completely redone in a client review. A gel taped to a light on day three of a five-day shoot is baked into every frame from that point forward — if the client hates the look in the edit, you're stuck with it.
  • Consistency across a long shoot. Matching a specific gel color exactly, day after day, on a multi-day production is genuinely hard — gel stock varies slightly batch to batch, tape and clips shift a gel's angle on a fixture, and small inconsistencies show up as shot-to-shot color drift that a grade has to fix anyway. A carefully built LUT or grade applied consistently in DaVinci Resolve is often more repeatable than physical gels re-rigged each day.
  • Speed under pressure. On a rushed run-and-gun shoot — the kind common in wedding cinematography and event work — there often isn't time to gel, test, and confirm a stylized look on set. Getting a clean, accurately white-balanced base and pushing the stylistic decision into the grade later is the more realistic workflow when the schedule doesn't allow for lighting experimentation.
  • Client and platform flexibility. Footage graded neutral-but-correct can be delivered in multiple styles for different platforms or edits — a moodier cut for a film festival submission, a brighter cut for social media — from the same source. Footage baked in-camera with a strong color effect locks you into one interpretation.

The Balanced Philosophy: Fix Foundations On Set, Push Style in the Grade

The working answer, and the one that holds up across most productions, is a division of labor between the set and the color bay:

DecisionWhere It BelongsWhy
Matching color temperature between key, fill, and background sourcesOn set, with CTB/CTOWhite balance is one global correction — mismatched Kelvin values in one frame can't be fixed cleanly afterward
Base exposure and dynamic rangeOn setClipped highlights and crushed shadows are unrecoverable information loss, no grade can invent detail that was never captured
A committed, intentional colored key/backlight that defines the scene's moodOn set, with effect gels, if the schedule allows testing and the look is locked creativelyReal colored light interacting with the environment looks more convincing than a digital wash on flat footage
Overall palette, split-toning, contrast shaping, filmic responseIn the gradeFully reversible, refinable across revisions, and consistent shot-to-shot without physical rigging variance
Subtle mood shifts, day-for-night pushes, look variations per platformIn the gradeKeeps the source footage flexible enough to serve multiple final looks from one shoot

In practice, this means: get your white balance right using CTB/CTO to match sources on set, expose so your highlights and shadows both hold real detail, and only then decide how much of the "look" you want baked in with an effect gel versus how much you want to build later with nodes, power windows, and a considered grade. A DOP who gels a strong color into a scene, but also protects clean exposure and a matched white balance underneath it, gives their colorist the best of both — a shot with real, believable colored light, that still has enough tonal information to be pushed further or corrected if needed.

On-set rule of thumb: If a color decision changes what the footage can become later — clipped highlights, a badly mismatched white balance, blown-out skin tones — fix it on set, no exceptions. If a color decision only changes what the footage currently looks like — an overall color mood, a stylistic tint, a contrast curve — it's safe to defer to the grade, and often wiser to.

Practical Gel Kit for a Working DOP

You don't need a wall of gel books to cover most real jobs. A practical, budget-conscious kit covers correction first, effect second:

  • Full CTB and full CTO — for flipping a source entirely between tungsten and daylight.
  • 1/2 and 1/4 CTB, 1/2 and 1/4 CTO — for partial matching, which you'll reach for more often than full strength in real mixed-lighting situations.
  • Plus Green / Minus Green — for correcting the green spike common in fluorescent and some LED practicals, a mismatch that CTB/CTO alone won't fix.
  • A small set of effect gels — one warm amber, one cool blue, and one saturated accent color (magenta, deep red, or teal, depending on the kind of work you do) covers the majority of stylized lighting requests without needing a full swatch book.

Gel clips or spring clamps, heat-resistant tape, and a way to trim gel sheets to size round out the kit. None of this needs to be expensive — the discipline of knowing when to reach for a correction gel versus an effect gel matters far more than how large the collection is.

Key Takeaways

  • CTB and CTO gels correct color temperature mismatches on set — this is a lighting fix, not a stylistic one, and it should happen before you roll, not after.
  • Effect gels bake a real colored light into the scene, which behaves more believably than a flat color wash added in post, but the decision is a commitment that's hard to undo later.
  • White balance, exposure, and dynamic range are foundations — get these right in-camera, because a grade cannot invent detail that was never captured.
  • Overall palette, mood, and stylistic pushes belong in the grade, where they stay reversible, refinable, and consistent across a whole project.
  • The balanced philosophy: fix what limits your options on set, and defer what only changes the current look to DaVinci Resolve.

Quick FAQ

Q: What's the difference between a correction gel and an effect gel?

Correction gels (CTB and CTO) fix color temperature mismatches between sources so everything in frame agrees on one Kelvin value. Effect gels are creative colors (magenta, deep blue, amber, and so on) used deliberately for mood and style, not to correct anything.

Q: Should I gel my lights or just fix the color in DaVinci Resolve?

Fix color temperature mismatches with CTB/CTO gels on set — white balance is a single global correction and can't cleanly fix two different color casts in one frame. Save stylistic color decisions, like overall palette and mood, for the grade, where they stay reversible.

Q: Can DaVinci Resolve replicate the look of an effect gel convincingly?

It can approximate the hue, but a real gelled light interacting with an actual environment behaves differently from a flat color pushed onto neutral footage in post, especially in highlight falloff and how the color wraps around edges and mixes with ambient light.

Q: What strength of CTB or CTO should I use to match tungsten to daylight?

A full CTB shifts roughly 3200K tungsten up toward 5600K daylight, and a full CTO shifts roughly 5600K daylight down toward 3200K tungsten. In practice you'll often need less than full strength, so keep 1/2 and 1/4 strengths on hand for partial matches.

Q: Is it ever a mistake to commit to a color effect gel on set?

Yes — on a rushed schedule, a multi-day shoot needing tight consistency, or a project where the client might want look flexibility later, it's often safer to keep the base footage clean and accurately white-balanced, then build the stylized color in the grade instead.
