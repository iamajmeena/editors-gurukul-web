The Complete Pre-Production Checklist for Solo Filmmakers

A solid pre-production checklist for solo filmmakers covers six stages: script/outline, shot list, location and permissions, gear and backup power/storage, shot prioritization for limited time, and a one-person call sheet — miss any one of these and the shoot day is where you pay for it.

Quick take: Most solo shoots don't fail because of bad footage — they fail because of skipped planning. Write an outline before you shoot, build a shot list you can actually finish in the time you have, scout and confirm permission for your location, charge and pack backups for everything that can die mid-shoot, and write yourself a one-page call sheet the night before. That's the whole system.

Why Solo Filmmakers Skip Pre-Production (And Why It Costs More Later)

When you're shooting alone — director, camera operator, gaffer, and sound recordist rolled into one person — pre-production is the first thing that gets cut when you're short on time. There's no producer nagging you for a shot list, no assistant director keeping you on schedule, no one double-checking that your batteries are charged. You are the entire crew, which means you are also the entire safety net, and if you skip planning, nobody catches the gap.

The irony is that solo shoots need more structure than crewed ones, not less. On a crewed set, if the director forgets to note a shot, the DOP or script supervisor might catch it. Solo, there's no second brain in the room. A missed shot on day one often means a return trip, a re-shoot, or a scene that just never gets covered properly in the edit. A proper pre-production checklist for solo filmmakers isn't bureaucracy — it's the only insurance you have against your own blind spots.

This guide walks through the six stages that solo creators skip most often, in the order you should actually do them, with a practical framework at each step and a simple call-sheet-equivalent you can fill out for yourself before every shoot.

The Six-Stage Pre-Production Framework

Here's the full checklist at a glance. Each stage gets its own section below with the practical detail.

StageWhat You're DoingSkip This And...
1. Script/OutlineLock the story beats or talking points before you touch a cameraYou shoot random footage and discover the gaps in the edit
2. Shot ListTranslate the outline into specific, filmable shotsYou waste time deciding what to shoot on location
3. Location & PermissionsScout the space, confirm you're legally allowed to shoot thereYou get shut down mid-shoot or shoot somewhere unsafe
4. Gear Check & BackupsTest every piece of gear, pack backup batteries and cardsA dead battery or full card ends your shoot day early
5. Shot PrioritizationRank your shot list by importance for time-constrained daysYou run out of light shooting low-priority coverage first
6. Solo Call SheetWrite yourself a one-page plan for the dayYou arrive on location and waste the first 30 minutes deciding what to do

Stage 1: Script or Outline — Lock the Story Before You Shoot

Even a five-minute YouTube video needs an outline. This doesn't have to be a formatted screenplay — for most solo creators it's a simple beat sheet: what happens first, second, third, and how the piece ends. What matters is that you know the shape of the story before you're standing on location trying to invent it in real time.

For narrative work, write a scene-by-scene outline with the core action and dialogue intent of each scene, even if the dialogue itself gets adjusted on the day. For talking-head or documentary-style content, write down your key points in the order you want to make them, plus any specific phrases or facts you need to say correctly — it's much easier to fix a line on paper than to notice a factual error three edits deep in DaVinci Resolve.

Pro tip: Write your outline, then read it out loud once, timing yourself. If a five-minute video outline takes you eight minutes to read at a normal pace, you're already over-scoped for a single shoot day. Cut before you shoot, not after.

Stage 2: Shot List — Turn the Outline Into Filmable Shots

The outline tells you what the story is. The shot list tells you what the camera needs to physically capture to tell it. This is the single most skipped step for solo creators, because it feels like an extra piece of paperwork between you and shooting — but it's the difference between arriving on location with a plan and arriving to "figure it out."

A usable shot list needs, at minimum: a shot number, a short description of the action or subject, the framing (wide, medium, close-up), and any camera movement. You don't need software for this — a simple table in a notes app or a printed sheet works fine.

#Shot DescriptionFramingMovement/Notes
1Establishing shot of workspaceWideStatic, tripod
2Hands typing / working detailClose-upStatic or slow push-in
3Subject talking to cameraMediumStatic, eye-line marked
4Cutaway of surroundingsWide/MediumHandheld, for B-roll flexibility

Stage 3: Location Scouting and Permissions

This is the stage solo creators skip most confidently, usually because "I've been to this place before" feels like scouting. It isn't. A proper scout answers questions a casual visit doesn't: where does the light fall at the actual time you'll be shooting, where are the power outlets if you need them, what's the ambient noise level at that hour, and — critically — are you actually allowed to film there.

  • Check permission requirements in advance. Public parks, malls, cafes, heritage sites, and most private properties have rules about filming, even for non-commercial content. A quick phone call or on-site question to management before your shoot day avoids a shutdown mid-scene.
  • Scout at the actual time of day you'll shoot. A location that looks great at noon can be unusable at 5 PM once direct sun turns into harsh side-light through a window, or once a quiet street becomes a traffic corridor.
  • Note power and washroom access. Solo shoots often run longer than planned. Knowing where you can charge a battery or take a break saves you from cutting the day short unnecessarily.
  • Have a backup location in mind. Weather, noise, or an unexpected closure can take your primary location off the table. A rough backup plan, even a mental one, keeps a shoot day from collapsing entirely.
Warning: Filming in a location without permission isn't just a legal risk — it's the fastest way to lose access to that location for future shoots. A polite ask beforehand almost always gets a better outcome than an apology afterward.

Stage 4: Gear Check and Backup Batteries/Cards

On a crewed set, there's usually a gear person whose entire job is making sure nothing dies mid-shoot. Solo, that job is yours, and it's the stage that punishes you hardest when skipped — because a dead battery or a full card doesn't just cost you time, it can end the shoot day outright if you don't have a spare.

Run this check the night before, not the morning of. You want time to charge or fix anything that's dead or broken, not discover it while you're already running late.

ItemCheckBackup to Pack
Camera batteriesFully charged, tested in-cameraAt least 2 spares, more for full-day shoots
Memory cardsFormatted in-camera, verified empty1-2 spare cards with enough free space for the whole day
LensesFront element clean, no fungus/dust spots visibleA lens cloth and blower in the bag at all times
Audio recorder/micBatteries fresh, levels tested with a real voiceSpare AA/AAA batteries or a charged backup mic battery
Tripod/stabilizerPlates tightened, legs lock properlyGaffer tape for any quick fix
Monitor/phone as monitorCharged, mounted, connected and testedA power bank in the bag

And if you're still deciding on the camera itself, our guide to choosing your first camera as a beginner filmmaker walks through matching a body to your actual use-case rather than chasing a spec sheet.

Pro tip: Label your spare batteries and cards with a simple numbering system, and physically move a battery or card to a separate pocket once it's used. On a long solo day, it's easy to lose track of which battery is charged and which is dead — a system beats memory every time.

Stage 5: Shot Prioritization for Time-Constrained Solo Shoots

Solo shoots almost always run out of time before they run out of shot list. You're setting up every shot yourself, checking your own framing by walking back and forth to the camera, and often re-doing takes because you were also the performer. A 20-shot list that would take a two-person team three hours can easily take a solo shooter five or six.

The fix isn't shooting faster — it's prioritizing before you start, so the shots you cut for time are the ones that matter least.

  1. Mark every shot as Essential, Important, or Nice-to-Have before you leave for location. Essential shots are the ones without which the story doesn't work at all — the interview answer, the key action beat. Nice-to-Have shots are texture and coverage that improve the edit but aren't load-bearing.
  2. Shoot Essential shots first, always, regardless of how the day is going. If light or time runs out, it should cost you polish, not story.
  3. Group shots by location and light, not by story order. If three shots from different points in your story all happen in the same corner of a room under the same light, shoot them back to back. Re-lighting and re-setting up costs more solo time than shooting out of sequence and reordering in the edit.
  4. Build in a hard time buffer. If you estimate a shot list takes four hours, plan for five. Solo shoots always have friction — a forgotten setting, a re-take, a location noise you have to wait out — that a crewed shoot would absorb across multiple people.

Stage 6: The Solo Call Sheet — A One-Page Plan for Yourself

A call sheet on a normal production tells every department where to be, when, and with what. Working alone, you still need that same discipline, just condensed to a single page you write for yourself the night before. This is the step that turns a vague plan into an actual schedule, and it takes ten minutes to fill out.

FieldWhat to Fill In
Location + addressExact spot, plus parking or entry notes
Arrival timeWhen you'll physically be on location, before setup starts
Shooting windowStart and hard-stop time, based on light or your own schedule
Shot list referenceLink or note to the prioritized shot list from Stage 5
Gear checklist statusConfirmed packed, charged, and tested the night before
Weather/light notesSunrise/sunset time if outdoors, forecast check
Contact/safety noteSomeone who knows where you are and roughly when you'll be back
Quick take: The safety-note field matters more than it sounds. If you're shooting alone in an unfamiliar or remote location, telling one person your plan and expected return time is a five-second habit that actually matters.

Print this or keep it as a note on your phone, and check it off field by field the morning of the shoot. The goal isn't paperwork for its own sake — it's removing every decision you'd otherwise have to make standing on location, so all your attention goes toward actually shooting.

Here's roughly how these six stages map onto a realistic solo shoot for a short video, spread across the days before the shoot itself.

None of this adds up to more than half a day of total prep spread across a week — a small investment against the cost of a wasted shoot day, a re-shoot, or footage that can't be cut into a finished piece because a key shot is missing.

Quick FAQ

Q: What's the minimum pre-production a solo filmmaker actually needs?

At minimum: a written outline of your story or key points, a shot list with framing noted for each shot, confirmed location permission, charged spare batteries and formatted cards, and a one-page plan for the day. Skipping any one of these is where solo shoots typically go wrong.

Q: How far in advance should I scout a location?

Ideally 2-4 days before the shoot, and at the same time of day you plan to shoot, so you see the actual light and noise conditions rather than a best-case visit at a random hour.

Q: How many spare batteries and cards should a solo shooter carry?

At least two spare camera batteries for a half-day shoot, more for a full day, plus one to two spare memory cards with enough combined free space to cover the entire shoot without needing to offload footage on location.

Q: What should go on a solo call sheet if I'm working alone?

Location and address, your arrival time, your shooting window with a hard stop, a reference to your prioritized shot list, confirmation that gear is packed and charged, light/weather notes, and a safety note telling someone else where you are and when to expect you back.

Q: How do I prioritize a shot list when I'm short on time?

Mark every shot as Essential, Important, or Nice-to-Have before you arrive on location. Shoot Essential shots first regardless of how the day is going, group remaining shots by location and light rather than story order, and build in a time buffer beyond your estimate.
