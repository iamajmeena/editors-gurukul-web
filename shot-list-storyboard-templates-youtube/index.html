Shot List and Storyboard Templates Every YouTube Creator Should Use

A shot list is a simple table you fill in before you shoot — shot number, framing, action, audio note, and status — and it is the single fastest way to cut hours off your edit. Most YouTube creators don't need a full storyboard; they need this.

Quick take: If you're making talking-head, vlog, or tutorial content, skip the storyboard and use a five-column shot list instead — shot number, framing, action, audio note, status. Save full storyboards for scripted narrative work, ads, and anything with a stunt, a VFX shot, or a tight multi-camera choreography that has to match frame-for-frame.

Why a Written Shot List Matters Even for "Simple" Content

Every editor has opened a project folder with forty unlabeled clips and felt their stomach drop. That folder is what happens when a creator decides the video is "simple enough" to shoot from memory. It never is. The moment you're covering a topic with more than two talking points, need one cutaway, and want a piece of b-roll to hide a jump cut, you have exceeded what your memory can reliably hold on a shoot day.

A shot list is not bureaucracy. It is a five-minute planning document that answers one question before you ever pick up the camera: what does this video need, shot by shot, to cut together cleanly? Skip that question on set, and you answer it in the edit bay instead — at a much higher cost, because now you're either re-shooting or compromising the cut around footage you don't have.

What a Shot List Actually Saves You

  • Time on set. You know exactly what to shoot next instead of standing there deciding. A 20-shot list can be knocked out in under an hour if you're not improvising the plan as you go.
  • Time in the edit. When every clip has a clear intended role before it's even recorded, you stop hunting for "something to cut to here" — you shot that something on purpose.
  • Coverage gaps. A shot list surfaces missing cutaways and missing audio before you've wrapped and packed the gear, not three days later when you're mid-edit and the location is gone.
  • Continuity. If you're shooting across two sessions — common for longer videos or when a guest's schedule forces a reshoot day — a written record of what you already have prevents duplicate or contradicting footage.

None of this requires artistic storyboarding. It requires a document, and honestly it can live in a notes app, a spreadsheet, or a printed sheet clipped to your camera cage. The format matters far less than the discipline of actually writing it before you shoot.

The Lightweight Shot List Template (Use This for 90% of YouTube Content)

Here is the exact structure I use on client shoots and recommend to every creator who asks how to stop losing time in post. Five columns, nothing more:

  1. Shot # — a simple sequential number so you and your editor can reference "shot 12" instead of describing it from scratch.
  2. Framing — wide, medium, close-up, over-the-shoulder, insert/macro. This is the one column that forces you to think about visual variety instead of shooting everything from the same eye-level medium shot.
  3. Action — what actually happens in the shot in one line. Not the whole script, just enough to remind you on set what you're capturing.
  4. Audio Note — is this a sync-sound shot (talking, needs clean audio), a silent b-roll shot (music/VO will cover it), or a shot that needs a specific sound effect or ambience recorded separately?
  5. Status — Not Shot / Shot / Needs Reshoot. This single column is what turns a shot list from a planning document into a working production tool you actually update during the day.

Pro tip: Add the Status column even if you think you won't need it. On any shoot longer than 15 minutes of setup, you will lose track of what's actually in the card. "Shot" vs "Not Shot" takes two seconds to mark and saves you from re-watching footage just to confirm coverage.

Storyboard vs. Shot List — What's the Real Difference

These two get used interchangeably by beginners, but they solve different problems.

StoryboardShot List
Visual — drawn or mocked-up frames showing exact compositionText-based — a table describing each shot in words
Built for narrative, scripted, or heavily choreographed sequencesBuilt for fast-turnaround content: vlogs, tutorials, talking-head, most YouTube formats
Communicates exact framing and blocking to a full crew (DOP, gaffer, actors)Communicates coverage needs to a one or two-person crew, often just yourself
Takes hours to draw properly, worth it when a scene needs precise visual continuityTakes minutes to write, worth it on almost every shoot
Essential when a shot involves stunts, VFX, multi-camera coverage, or a specific emotional beat that has to land visuallyEssential when you just need to make sure you don't forget a cutaway

Full storyboards earn their place on branded ad shoots, short films, and anything with scripted actors hitting specific marks — situations where the visual composition itself is part of the story being told, and where a director needs to communicate that composition to a DOP and a crew before anyone touches a camera. If that's the kind of project you're planning, your pre-production needs go well beyond a shot list — worth reading through a full pre-production checklist for solo filmmakers before you get to set.

For the overwhelming majority of YouTube content — talking-head videos, tutorials, vlogs, review videos, even most narrative-style shorts shot solo — a shot list gets you 90% of the planning benefit in 10% of the time. You don't need to draw anything. You need to know what you're shooting and why.

Building Your Shot List From the Script or Outline

The fastest way to build a shot list is to go through your script or bullet-point outline line by line and ask three questions for each section:

  • Does this need me talking to camera, or can it be covered with b-roll and voiceover? Sections that are purely explanatory are often better as b-roll + VO — faster to record, easier to re-record if you flub a line.
  • What visual would make this point land faster than me just saying it? This is where cutaways and inserts come from — a close-up of a setting being changed, a product being held up, a before/after comparison.
  • Where will the cut probably need to hide a jump? Any time you're delivering the same point in more than one take, or condensing a longer explanation, you'll need a cutaway to cut away to. Plan for it now instead of discovering the jump cut in the edit with nothing to cover it.

If you're shooting solo and directing yourself in front of the camera, this planning step matters even more, because you don't have a second person on set catching missed coverage in real time. It's worth pairing your shot list with a proper solo filmmaking workflow for shooting, directing, and acting alone so the two systems reinforce each other.

Your gear also shapes what's realistic to put on the shot list. A single-camera creator switching between a wide establishing shot and a close-up insert needs time between setups to physically move the camera and reframe, while a two-camera setup can capture both angles in the same take. If you're still deciding what body and lens combination fits your shooting style, it's worth reading through how to choose your first camera as a beginner filmmaker before you lock in a shot list that assumes gear you don't actually own yet.

How This Actually Saves Editing Time

The payoff of a shot list isn't really on set — it's in the edit, and it compounds in a few specific ways:

  • You stop scrubbing for footage that doesn't exist. Editors waste enormous amounts of time searching for "the shot that would fix this cut," not realizing it was never captured. A shot list with a Status column tells you, before you even open the project, exactly what coverage you have.
  • Your bins organize themselves. Name your files or clips against your shot numbers on set (even just verbally slating "shot 4, take 2" on camera) and your edit import becomes a matching exercise instead of a guessing game.
  • You cut with intention instead of improvising a structure from raw footage. When every clip already has an intended role — hook, insert, cutaway, wrap-up — you're assembling a plan, not discovering the video for the first time in the timeline.
  • Reshoots get isolated instead of derailing the whole edit. If shot 6 needs a reshoot, that's a five-minute pickup, not a reason to re-cut half the sequence around a missing piece.

If you're grading and finishing in DaVinci Resolve afterward, well-organized, intentionally-shot footage also makes your color and audio pass faster — you're not trying to rescue a mismatched, unplanned edit at the finishing stage, you're polishing footage that was already built to cut together.

Pro tip: Keep a running shot list template as a duplicate-able document — spreadsheet, notes app, or even a paper template you photocopy. The five columns rarely need to change between projects. What changes is the content in each row.

Adapting the Template for Different YouTube Formats

The core five-column structure holds across formats, but what you emphasize shifts slightly:

  • Talking-head / tutorial: Lean heavily on the Audio Note column — most of your shots are sync-sound, and your inserts (screen recordings, product close-ups) need to be flagged clearly as silent so you don't waste time worrying about mic placement on shots that don't need it.
  • Vlogs: Add a rough time-of-day or location note if you're shooting across a full day — a shot list doubles as a loose schedule so you don't backtrack across a location.
  • Review / product videos: Framing becomes critical — plan explicit close-up/insert shots for every feature you intend to call out, since these are the shots viewers actually remember.
  • Client or wedding-style event coverage: The shot list becomes a checklist of must-get moments rather than a fully controlled shoot, since you can't direct a live event the way you can a solo piece to camera. If that's your work, it's worth cross-referencing against a dedicated wedding shoot day workflow and gear checklist so nothing gets missed under time pressure.

Whatever format you're shooting, the discipline is the same: write it down before you shoot, mark status as you go, and treat the Status column as seriously as the shot itself. A shot list only saves you time if you actually update it on set — a shot list you fill in once and never touch again is just a wish list.

A Note on B-Roll Planning

Your Action and Framing columns are also where you plan deliberate b-roll coverage instead of grabbing random cutaways at the end of a shoot day. Thinking through your b-roll shots in advance — rather than improvising them after the main content is in the can — is one of the fastest ways to make a simple video feel more expensive than its budget. If b-roll is a weak spot in your current process, it's worth going deeper on dedicated b-roll shooting techniques for a more expensive look and folding those shot types directly into your shot list template as standing rows you reuse on every shoot.

Storyboarding Tools, If You Do Need One

For the narrative or high-production cases where a full storyboard earns its place, you don't need elaborate drawing skill. Stick-figure frames with an arrow for camera movement and a one-line action note communicate the same information as a polished illustration — the goal is clarity for your crew, not gallery-quality art. Free storyboard templates are widely available as simple grids of empty frames with caption lines underneath; the value is in the thinking you do filling them in, not the software you use to make them.

If you're new to planning shoots at all, and you're not yet sure whether your project needs a shot list or a full storyboard, it usually comes down to one test: if you can describe every shot in one sentence and shoot it solo without a crew, a shot list is enough. If you need to explain exact composition, blocking, or timing to other people before anyone touches a camera, invest the time in a storyboard instead.

Quick FAQ

Q: Do I really need a shot list for a simple YouTube video?

Yes, even for a single-person talking-head video. The moment you add one cutaway, one b-roll insert, or one screen recording, you have more than one shot to track — and a written list, even a five-line one, prevents gaps that only get discovered in the edit.

Q: What's the difference between a shot list and a storyboard?

A shot list is a text-based table describing each shot's framing, action, audio, and status. A storyboard is a visual sequence of drawn or mocked-up frames showing exact composition. Most YouTube content only needs a shot list; storyboards are worth the extra time for scripted narrative, ads, or heavily choreographed multi-camera scenes.

Q: What columns should a basic shot list include?

Shot number, framing (wide/medium/close-up), action (what happens in one line), audio note (sync sound, silent, or specific SFX), and status (Not Shot, Shot, Needs Reshoot). These five cover almost every YouTube format.

Q: Can I use a shot list for solo, unscripted vlogging?

Yes — even a loose outline turned into five or six rows helps. You don't need every shot pre-planned in a vlog, but flagging your must-get establishing shots and key cutaways in advance still saves real editing time.

Q: How do I make sure my shot list actually gets used on set?

Keep it physically accessible while shooting — printed, on a clipboard, or open on a phone or tablet next to your camera — and actually update the Status column as you shoot. A shot list you write once and never look at again during the shoot provides almost none of the benefit.
